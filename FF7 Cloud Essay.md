# The Assassination of Cloud Strife: How the *FFVII* Remakes Murdered a Hero and Left His Corpse a Psychopath

The core tragedy of Cloud Strife was never meant to be a loud display of psychosis. In the 1997 original, Cloud’s struggle was a beautifully designed sci-fi identity crisis grounded in strict biological rules. He was a hero struggling with physical brain trauma, not a psychopath engineered for shock value. His mind, in ruins, was reconstructed by the words of a dying friend, not further fractured by the overt machinations of an alien parasite. His identity was anchored to the daring salvation of a darling friend, not confounded by convenient 'whispers' from the writer's pen. His story was a quiet, high-functioning, and deeply human ordeal that relied entirely on communal teamwork to overcome an extraterrestrial horror capable of destroying the entire world.

The *Remake* trilogy systematically subverts this monument to human agency in favor of cinematic sensationalism. Its modern interpretation pathologizes an elegant sci-fi phenomenon into a loud, theatrical meltdown, prioritizing immediate visual shock over long-term narrative logic. By transforming the epic struggle of Cloud's internal battle into the violent outbursts of a continuous public liability, the new games inflict catastrophic damage not just to his character, but to the entire cast itself, turning what should be a highly competent group of rebels into a network of negligent babysitters.

## From Silent Trauma to Cinematic Seizure

To understand this narrative collapse, we must look at how the remakes fundamentally alter the sensory presentation of Cloud's trauma. In the 1997 classic, his lapses of thought were quiet, internal, and abstractly presented via text boxes and high-pitched cues. This allowed Cloud to appear fully composed to his allies, while players drew secret insights from his innermost thoughts. The original text did not present Cloud as a volatile risk, but as a competent leader suffering from amnesia induced by Mako-poisoning, and doubly impacted by alien cellular infection. When not actively experiencing a memory conflict, he was charismatic, efficient, and reliable. The worst you could have accused the man of was that he sometimes gets a migraine. Well, that and he aggressively demanded a paycheck before agreeing to save the planet.

The *Remake* trilogy rejects these subtle boundaries by turning his trauma into a continuous cinematic spectacle bursting with unmissable physical seizures and distorted camera effects galore. This shift completely upends the neurological context of his condition. Cloud's amnesia was originally a severe Traumatic Brain Injury (TBI) caused by extreme exposure to Mako, a process that normally destroys a person's mind entirely. In the original logic, Cloud survived this terminal threat not once but twice, with the help of two distinct human anchors: Zack Fair, who unintentionally provided an external blueprint of his own life by talking continuously to Cloud as he recovered from his catatonic state, and Tifa Lockhart, who brought him back from the brink of annihilation, using their combined memories of a shared childhood to anchor him to his actual past.

Cloud's adoption of the SOLDIER persona was a matter of **confabulation**, a biological symptom of severe TBI where the mind automatically fills in the blanks using whatever external physical objects may be at hand. Zack literally dressed his friend's catatonic body in a uniform not his own, giving his fractured mind upon waking a raw and powerful clue. In fact all the objects, the uniform, the sword, they are far from some ego-driven facade Cloud chooses to hide his identity crisis away with. They are instead taken as evident and obvious facts of life. Even his dead yet tragically unremembered comrade at arms served merely as a matter of course.

In such a frail state as this, it's only later that we see him come to some semblance of a whole person again when he and Tifa catch up at the sector 7 train station. Such a powerfully familiar and yet distinctly aged and adult face as his old childhood crush would easily help fill in the rest of the gaps, electrifying his framented mind into some sort of coherent whole. Despite Tifa's original misgivings and concern for his health, his naturally charming and core personality would once again became a thriving and dominant spark that moved a man with critical brain injury into becoming a first-class EX-SOLDIER turned mercenary, "EX" because he was alone, and mercenary, because he had no money. Lurking below the surface we are left to imagine how his parasitical infestation might have played some role in all of this as well.

Conversely, the remakes sacrifice internal character logic to serve a broader meta-sequel gimmick. Cloud is allowed to remain the active leader as he drops in and out of forced ineptitude, likely caused by a sudden onset of writer's flair, completely shattering the 4th wall, affording no credibility to its updated and sequelized narrative's logic. In 1997, Cloud possessed strong social and structural capability, such as delivering a complex, hours-long history lesson in Kalm with perfect composure before his crew, perfectly attuned to his somber yet captivating story, reflecting the social atmosphere of the group. The party themselves throughout ask clarifying questions with deep empathy and respect. They react with sorrow and awe as Cloud masterfully describes wtih the gift of an orator Sephiroth's slip and descent into madness. The group treats him with immense dignity, hanging onto his every word as the threat of what they are chasing dawns on them.

The singular moment where his memory hits a blank space, leaving him unable to remember the end of his own story, is treated with confusion and surprise, but the party attributes it to standard trauma rather than the actual underlying brain injury. His humanity in the moment is so genuine that everyone lets it go out of solemn respect, considering the weight of the story as heard much more the burden of what it must have been to have lived it.

Throughout the story, his condition is complicated yet further, by much more visible mid-game struggles not caused by general bouts of hallucination, but rather by biological overrides Jenova cells controlled by his greatest enemy inflict upon his mind. The threat of which only becomes apparent when their foe is lurking within close proximity. This is not only explictily visualized, but verily proven when the player must control a manifestation of Cloud's inner child, desperately trying and failing to stop his own body from handing over the Black Materia to Sephiroth at the Temple of the Ancients. It is further proven at the Forgotten City altar, where Cloud's core consciousness actively fights against an external command to strike his faithful ally Aerith.

Finally, when Cloud fully succumbs to the Jenova 'Reunion' at the Northern Crater, the original text respects this biological reality. It takes the most drastic and sensible action possible by removing him from the party entirely, forcing Cid Highwind to assume the mantle of captain while Tifa Lockhart becomes Cloud's dutiful caretaker. The remakes completely fracture this balance by forcing a hardened resistance faction to blindly cede military command to a consistently visible liability, at no point even acknowledging the potential cost to their own cause.

## From Acute Emergency to Chronic Liability

This travesty of a modern trilogy becomes undeniable when contrasted with the logic of the original. In particular, *Rebirth’s* early-game escalations, culminating with an egregious Gongaga Reactor incident, are an unbearable weight too heavy for a narrative already too inconsistent to hold.

### Acute Emergency
In the original text, Cloud’s surrender of the Black Materia and his subsequent physical assault on Aerith swell into an imminent, catastrophic medical emergency. Prior to the Temple of the Ancients, Cloud operates with a flawless track record of tactical execution and reliable leadership. Because his violence quickly surges into a shocking climax through the events of the temple, the party’s response is logically sound and instant the moment he breaks.

As soon as Sephiroth's psychic paralysis lifts, no matter the companion the player has chosen for their party, they react with a singular, unified instinct. Though their individual dialogue lines vary slightly depending on the party makeup, they all scream for Cloud to stop his vicious attack. When words fail, they pay violence with violence, physically battering their own leader until he is limp and unconscious in order to protect Aerith. It is a desperate attempt to bring him to his senses, opting to take those senses from him completely as they realize the impossibility of anything less.

This sudden whiplash is so jarring that the trauma grabs hold of the player themselves. It is completely unexpected. At no point does the story suggest such a betrayal is possible. Cloud has just viciously attacked a favored ally, a woman he has personally taken pains to save and to protect, turning against her in an outburst equaled in lunacy only by his surrender of the Black Materia to their sworn enemy.

At this stage, while the team knows of Jenova, they do not yet understand the horror of the alien parasite vying for Cloud's soul. They treat the event strictly as an acutely externalized mental hijack triggered entirely by Sephiroth's terrifying presence, which they all personally feel. When Cloud's biological override triggers and he hands over the Black Materia, his teammates can all see the strings being pulled by the master puppeteer right in front of their eyes, with Cloud presumably suffering the most violent breakdown simply by being closest in physical proximity to their enemy.

The narrative actively pauses for an intervention. After they flee for safety and Cloud recovers in Gongaga, he does not try to hide the truth. Instead, he is initially consumed by deep shame and avoidant despair, turning away and refusing to look at his friends. When he finally does speak, he openly confesses his instability to Barret and Tifa. This honesty forces the group to confront his vulnerability, but it also shatters their trust. Barret insists he wants Cloud off the team for Aerith's safety, relenting only because they need Cloud to track her down.

Cloud bravely takes responsibility for his actions, making sure they all understand that there is a clear and present danger moving ever closer as they hound after Sephiroth. He asserts that they must stop him by any means necessary if he loses control again, effectively putting his life in their hands. This act shows that even in his lowest moment, Cloud's innate sense of leadership fiercely prioritizes the safety of his comrades above himself, even at the expense of his very life.

This logic holds firm during the ultimate tragedy at the Forgotten City altar. When Cloud draws his sword and trembles violently, trying to stop himself from harming Aerith, his team watches with horror as it agonizingly dawns on them that Sephiroth must be physically near. After the earth-shattering tragedy unfolds, they can rationalize the breakdown because the enemy is actively pulling the puppet strings right in front of them. Instead of giving up on their leader, it makes complete sense to them that they must help a compromised friend fight off an active assault, just as they must fight off an evil predator trying to devour their planet.

This hard-earned loyalty reveals their deep compassion for a man who, even when subjected to total psychic domination, still struggles mightily to resist while the world crumbles around them. Far from viewing him as a liability, the party implicitly understands the terrifying scale of their enemy's power and correctly recognizes Cloud as their greatest asset. None of them are aware of the parasite festering within him, but from their perspective, they know that if any one of them were in the same position, they would have turned their weapon upon Aerith without so much as a flinch.

They stand by their leader because they recognize that if anyone has the sheer strength of will to withstand the howling malice of Sephiroth’s magic, it would have to be Cloud. This unwavering bond is explicitly cemented in the wake of tragedy when he resolutely declares his intent to hunt Sephiroth down. His absolute clarity of heart, mind, and will inspires them to move forward, proving that even in their darkest hour, he remains a beacon of absolute resolve who will lead them to the literal ends of the earth.

### Chronic Liability
The remake trilogy destroys this logical economy by making Cloud’s psychological decay the daily baseline. From the earliest chapters, he is depicted swinging his sword at thin air, shouting at 'Whispers' his teammates cannot see, inexplicably refusing to explain himself, and exhibiting severe psychotic breaks in full view of the public despite Sephiroth being miles away. To characters denied the player's meta-knowledge, this 'Mako-junkie' (yes, a citizen literally calls him that) no longer looks like a sane mercenary, much less a sane commander targeted by a localized writer's curse. He looks like a permanently unhinged, self-generating liability whose behavior should rightfully incur a mountain of lawsuits and property damage claims before he can even make it out of Sector 7.

This structural erosion reaches a point of no return at the Gongaga Reactor in *Rebirth*. When Sephiroth manipulates Cloud, the game explicitly shows that the villain is completely invisible to Tifa and the rest of the party. To the cast, there are no visible puppet strings. They are not witnessing a tragic biological override. Instead, they are simply watching their military commander attempt to execute murder on his lifelong childhood friend, slamming his sword down to violently blow Tifa off the platform and into a Mako reactor.

In the 1997 original, Cloud's flashpoint at the Temple is a solitary, catastrophic crescendo that instantly fractures the party's trust, leading to an agonizing intervention. Conversely, the remakes choose to dilute the narrative by transforming Cloud into a recurring, active threat long before the Temple even arrives. By allowing him to attempt literal murder with zero consequences, the story permanently destroys the cast’s credibility. Barret, Tifa, and Aerith cease to function as a competent team. Instead, they are reduced to negligent babysitters, blindly ceding military command to a man who just tried to execute one of their own.

*Rebirth* treats Cloud's maniacal assault on Tifa with jaw-dropping negligence. The party never holds a group intervention or questions his fitness to carry a grotesque, oversized cleaver. Instead, the horrific event is swept under the rug of a serialized romance, reducing tragedies to become the cheap tropes of a petty slasher flick. But don't worry, this is all sufficiently explained by a private conversation where a smiling Tifa simply promises to protect her own assailant, and thus all is forgiven, never speak of it again. By trading logical group accountability for manufactured melodrama, the writers prioritize immediate cinematic shock over basic psychological logic, rendering a legendary rebel faction complicit in the rampages of their own leader’s unchecked madness.

By normalizing Cloud's violence in the remakes, his final breakdown at the Forgotten City altar loses its abject terror. In 1997, it was the horrifying, shocking, unexpected climax of a villain exploiting a good man's vulnerability. In the modern blueprint, it is merely the predictable consequence of a group of characters ignoring a screaming, chronic red flag for dozens of hours for the sake of pop cinema.

## From Companions to Appliances

To understand how completely the modern trilogy excretes the logic of its cast, look at what it does to its two primary female leads. In the 1997 original, Tifa Lockhart and Aerith Gainsborough are not brainless cheerleaders designed to mindlessly validate a deteriorating protagonist. They are believable, mature human allies bound to Cloud through shared trauma and rationality.

The remakes completely slash away at these characters to a false core. By transforming what was originally a hidden Affection mechanic intended for laughs, as Barret would happily attest, and updating the romance mini-game into a full side-quest complete with floating emojis and obvious dialogue options, the remakes completely squish these girls into the same prison where Cloud was exploited during his infamous dance numbers at the Honey Bee Inn.

The sheer hypocrisy of endlessly praising Cloud's sequence as a "progressive triumph" of "self-expression," completely blinds the complicit killers to the blood on their hands, as they cheer for a brain-damaged veteran being forced into a cabaret for their amusement. Yet the moment the camera focuses on Tifa or Aerith, they cry foul play and allege a series of offenses, utterly blind to the fact that they just celebrated Cloud’s own exploitation. And here we sit, all of us losing three of the greatest characters of all time. Whether it's forcing Cloud to execute sexy stage routines or reducing Tifa and Aerith into competing corporate sex-bots, the result is identical. It trades a grounded sci-fi tragedy for a glorified dollhouse, ensuring that no matter how many times Cloud maniacally swings a massive sword in the faces of Tifa or Aerith, the girls remain eternally available.

### The Gaslit Survivor
Tifa’s position in the original story highlights the terrifying reality of dealing with a neurologically compromised leader. When Cloud’s condition is framed as a traumatic brain injury accompanied by severe confabulation, her behavior makes perfect sense. Confabulation explains exactly how Cloud can lie so flawlessly without actually being a liar. A patient who confabulates creates entirely false memories with absolutely no intent to deceive. They genuinely believe their fabricated reality. And because Cloud isn't actively lying, he displays none of the behavioral tells, eye shifts, or hesitations of a deceptive person. Instead, his delivery of the past is filled with the absolute conviction of empirical truth, maybe even pinned with an unwitting prophetic stare establishing a false authority over the past. As we shall see, this leaves Tifa completely defenseless and caught in a psychological nightmare where she cannot even trust her own memories, compelled by Cloud's natural talents for leadership malfunctioning as weapons of utterly convincing deceit.

Her initial silence during the Kalm backstory reveal is not some strategic mind game or an attempt to coddle a fragile friend. It is the direct fallout of a flawlessly constructed delusion. When Cloud relates the Nibelheim tragedy, his confabulated narrative is so detailed, precise, and structurally flawless that it effectively gaslights Tifa into doubting her own eyes and memories. After all, she suffered her own near-fatal trauma and a subsequent coma during those events. But because Cloud possesses hyper-specific, intimate information that only a firsthand witness could possibly know, she genuinely begins to lose confidence in her own senses. Tifa reasons that she must be the one remembering the past incorrectly, crushed under the terrifying weight of an anomaly she cannot comprehend and is afraid to confront.

When she later confesses in the Lifestream that she hid the truth because she 'didn't know what would happen' and 'didn't know what to do,' she is expressing the raw, paralyzed confusion of a trauma survivor. She is not playing mind games with a childhood friend. She stands back, utterly bewildered by the total certainty of Cloud’s neurological override making her question her own sanity. Tifa isn't dealing with a deceptive peer, but a biologically rewired brain operating with absolute, terrifying conviction. Through its very horror, this picture completely vindicates these two epic friends, allies, and even lovers of any wrongdoing on either of their parts. They prove how a friendship bound through fierce loyalty, trust, and battle-scarred hearts can defeat even the most intimate of enemies: themselves.

The remake trilogy completely betrays the psychological honesty and humility of her character. The script attempts to cover its crimes by giving Tifa a brief moment of defensive flutter. Right before he attacks her, she vocally defends her identity, and after nu-Cloud ineptly blunders his murderous assault, the next morning she explicitly confronts him about the fact that he just threw her into a Mako reactor, as one does. It should now be obvious how this inclusion only further exposes the utter bankruptcy of the remakes' writing. By forcing Tifa to openly tell Cloud that his memory is broken and that she doubts his identity, in one fell inconsequential swoop, the epic adventure of self-discovery is prematurely and permanently destroyed. The grand revelation that made the original narrative so powerful is so much trash tossed away along with the rest of the characters and their convictions. It entirely robs the story of Tifa's agonizing restraint, completely draining the mystery and tension out of the beautiful and blossoming relationship they once shared in the original game.

Instead of allowing her realistic terror to trigger even so much as a minor group chit-chat about a murderer in their midst, the narrative immediately deflates her agency to serve a choice-based relationship minigame. The writers force her to smile, pivot into a tender moment, and promise to protect her own assailant. Whereas originally a terrified and unconfident Tifa might acquiesce to a prophetic Cloud, who overtly wants nothing more than her well-being, *Rebirth* reduces her multi-layered trauma into a series of completely unrelated romance prompts, flattening a beautifully complex survivor into a compliant prop whose only purpose is to handwave an attempted execution and remain a pleasant option on the player's dating app.

### The Ghost in the Gestures
If Tifa's tragedy is defined by her silence, then Aerith’s is defined by Zack's echo. Her relationship with Cloud represents the profound heartbreak of falling in love with a ghost. In the 1997 classic, her attraction to him is not a mystical twist of fate or some pre-destined fairy tale like whispers on a stale wind. It is a deeply human, bittersweet accident born entirely out of nostalgia for a long lost love, a love ever so imperfect in its imitation and mystique, yet a genuine affection nonetheless. Aerith is drawn to Cloud because his rewired, post-traumatic TBI mind relies on the physical mannerisms, speech, and bravado of Zack Fair, which he subconsciously cataloged during that fatal truck ride back to Midgar. Cloud's unsuspecting mind copies Zack's personality even down to the squats he so vigorously performs on the way home. The original text explicitly establishes that Zack mentions his love for Aerith. And through Cloud's imperfect assimilation, this leaves him in the serendipitous disposition of both not knowing who Aerith is and yet simultaneously feeling comfortable, attracted, and quite familiar with her, almost as if she's a lost memory caught on the tip of his tongue. But his adoption of the SOLDIER persona reflected to Aerith a man she once knew and loved intimately, whom she knew to be dead. Unbeknownst to her, she was seeing the faded but still living blueprint of her long lost love, yet brought back from the grave, for fate had given her a second chance to once again fall in love.

When Cloud crashes into the Sector 5 church, he is projecting that echo everywhere he goes, immediately and dramatically catching her attention in a way she couldn't ignore. And as she openly notes at the Sector 6 playground, it is the physical composition of his body, the way he walks, stands, and carries himself, that mirrors her lost boyfriend. Cloud’s confabulation inadvertently creates a flawless social camouflage. Yet, as we find much later in the story, Aerith's sharp emotional intelligence allows her to see right through this mask. During their iconic date at the Gold Saucer, she explicitly tells Cloud that she wants to meet his true self, looking past Zack's stolen persona to find the real person buried beneath. Cloud's confused response, that he doesn't quite know what she means and that he's right in front of her shows the tragedy of a man who truly doesn't know who he is, and yet elevates Aerith to nothing less than a profoundly elegant and charming young woman, capable of poking at the depths of insanity itself, which Tifa was too afraid to even try. Aerith does it with such grace that it is only when it is too late that Cloud could ever realize the meaning behind her words, and that Tifa would finally learn from her example.

A part of me sighs, and I think any sane reader suspects why. The remake trilogy completely obliterates the brilliant balance of these characters by thoroughly bricking the entire process right from the start. At the Sector 8 street corner, the 1997 original presents a brief, three-line encounter featuring no magic, no sparks, and no neurological lapses. As far as we know, Cloud's brain is operating perfectly at its baseline, and as a player you would have no reason to suspect anything special about the flower girl, no more than any of the other seemingly incidental characters encountered. The remake, conversely, bloats this meeting into a slow motion supernatural event where Sephiroth manifests as a hallucination, and farts from the writer's pen physically swarm the characters for instant trailer bait.

This thematic collapse peaks at the church scene. In 1997, Aerith's attraction is grounded entirely in the fantastic events of her otherwise ordinary life: a handsome stranger comes crashing through the roof of her church, seemingly safeguarded by the very flowers she diligently grew, as if she had planted them there for him in the first place. And by real-world observation of Cloud's stolen posture, he likewise steals her heart as well as her attention. Conversely, in the modern blueprint Aerith is granted meta-aware future knowledge via Ancient magic, and it's literally as dry as that sentence sounds. The script at this point is about as authentically bound to its world rules as this essay, and completely breaks the fourth wall for the sake of a "meta-sequel". She treats Cloud from their first hours as if with a knowing wink at the camera, reacting entirely to a timeline script. Rather than becoming enamored by his physical behavior echoing a long lost love over the natural course of the game, she cryptically confesses her timeline-spoiler secrets by the time we arrive in her old Shinra prison room, operating clairvoyantly as an oracle actively concealing cosmic truths from her allies. And seemingly nothing at this point could matter more except that we await the oh-so-graciously given plot, whenever the writers feel the urge to write the next game.

This creates an environment where basic trust is utterly impossible. Just as Cloud’s unprovoked violence destroys his credibility as a commander, Aerith’s calculated secrecy destroys her credibility as a teammate. The remake leaves the party entirely stranded between two compromised extremes: a leader who actively tries to execute them, and a mystic who refuses to tell them why. The human element of happenstance and the tragic reality of Cloud's injury are both simultaneously destroyed without so much as a whimper why. Aerith is no longer a normal woman tragically drawn to the physical echoes of a forlorn love. Instead, she is reduced to a mystic following a corporate script, turning a masterpiece of humanly epic proportions into a hollow, conversational epitaph.

## Denied Rebirth: From a Eulogy to an Autopsy
A dignified sci-fi tragedy has been transformed into a frustrating spectacle of mental melodrama. In 1997, Cloud Strife was never meant to be an aloof, brooding loner. He was an awkward, introverted, yet deeply reliable guy who people looked up to. He happened to suffer from severe traumatic brain injury. But it was his leadership, his bravery, his athleticism, his love, his loyalty, even his desire for revenge, these are what defined him. Not his illness. But now that a titan of gaming has been brutally assaulted, murdered, and his corpse defiled, we are left with an unpredictable psychopath *solely* defined by his illness. Look at all that we've lost.

This infatuation with mental illness has brought catastrophic damage upon the entire franchise, worse than anything Meteor could ever do. It completely breaks world logic by forcing a hardened rebel vanguard to cede tactical command to a maniac who is known to be actively hallucinating. It completely liquidates the credibility of the cast. Companions are now tools only to be used and abused. Tifa Lockhart has been stripped of her status as a survivor, Aerith Gainsborough has been robbed of her office as advisor. Critics continue to cheer as each character is extinguished, one after another. Who needs a villain like Sephiroth, when this is all that is left?

Ultimately, these remakes reduce a tragic, triumphant celebration of peak human performance into a spectacle of helpless clinical weakness. The original classic functioned as a eulogy, a somber, deeply respectful meditation on human grief, memory loss, and the collective effort to save the world at great personal cost. The modern trilogy is a corporate autopsy. It dissects what was a living, breathing masterpiece into harvested parts for cheap tricks. By leaving behind the profound subtleties of human psychology, the remakes did not revitalize a legend. They murdered a hero, left his corpse a psychopath, and pawned a story that is no longer human. In the wake of Cloud Strife's assassination, his enemies danced on his corpse, the coffins of his allies, and Shinra finally won. So it's left to us to remember him for who he and his friends were, or perhaps even to discover them for the very first time.
